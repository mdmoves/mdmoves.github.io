Earlier this year, with the spring approaching and the weather getting warmer, I put aside my car and dusted off my old bike.

On my everyday commute, I came to appreciate how efficient, economical and sustainable a way of getting around it was—and how it always put me in such a good mood. 

As I was using it so much, I began thinking about upgrading my bike.

## Choosing the Right Bicycle Type

The "perfect" bike does not exist, and one must optimize it around the intended use.

All I knew was that I wanted a bike with a dropbar, comfortable and efficient on the long distances, yet robust and reliable for everyday use.

I wanted it to feel smooth and lightweight when ridden at a sustained pace on asphalt roads, yet secure on urban pavement or the many forest path shortcuts I take on my everyday commute.

First I searched the second-hand market, and discovered the great variety of bikes out there. Among gravel and road bikes of many models and brands, I couldn't find one that was perfect for me. So I decided to build my own.

This article series documents the process of building a bicycle starting from scratch, with no prior experience. I'll go through the design choices, compromises, and lessons learned along the way, covering everything from frame geometry and material selection, to the assembly and the final bike fit.

## First Design Choices

The first decisions when it comes to design a bike are its **geometry**, **material**, **groupset** and **tires**.

I optimized the bicycle design around these principles:
1. Durability and long-term reliability of the components.
2. Comfortable riding position.
3. Efficiency on asphalt roads.
4. Ability to withstand urban pavement and rugged terrain.
5. Aesthetics (as always).

This led to the following design choices:
1. **Endurance geometry**: allowing efficient riding position, yet relaxed enough for longer trips.
2. **Titanium frame**: time-less material, retaining excellent mechanical properties over time.
3. **Mechanical road groupset**: smooth and reliable Shimano Ultegra mechanical groupset, and disc brakes to allow wider tires.
4. **Tire width 32--40 mm**: road tires, wide enough to absorb vibrations and provide grip on light gravel.
5. **Clean look**: polished metal frame, black components, no visible brands.

This position the bike somewhere between an endurance road bike and a gravel—something I call an **"all-road"** bicycle.
